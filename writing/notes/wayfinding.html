Wayfinding

Wayfinding joins environmental cues with a person’s evolving mental route.

AI-generated exampleThis is substantive prototype content written to test the linked-reading interface, not Avery’s published writing.

Wayfinding is more than following signs. It is the ongoing work of answering three questions: where am I, where can I go, and how will I recognize progress? Signs help, but so do sightlines, thresholds, landmarks, and the shape of the route itself.

Digital navigation often overvalues destination selection and undervalues orientation. A click succeeds technically when the next page loads, yet the reader may not understand how the new place relates to the old one. Interface navigation needs continuity as much as choice.

Good wayfinding uses redundancy. A station has a name, a color, a position on a line, and a physical environment. Likewise, a note can have a title, URL, visual position, and visible ancestry. These cues cooperate to build spatial memory.

Maps offer overview; routes offer sequence; landmarks support recognition. No single representation answers every navigation question. The interesting design problem is deciding which one should remain present during reading.
