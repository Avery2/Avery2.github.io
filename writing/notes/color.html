Color

Color is relational: what a color does depends on what surrounds it.

Color names encourage us to think in isolated swatches: blue, vermilion, gray. Perception is less obedient. A color changes character beside another color, across a larger area, or under a different expectation. The practical unit of color is therefore a relationship.

In interfaces, color can group, rank, warn, and create rhythm. But color is most reliable when it reinforces another cue. A red error state also needs language; a selected item benefits from shape or weight. This is not timidity. It recognizes that perception varies with vision, display, lighting, and context.

Designers often ask whether a palette is harmonious before asking whether its distinctions do useful work. Contrast is the bridge between aesthetics and function. It determines whether text can be read, whether layers separate, and whether the eye can find the next action.

Color also carries learned meanings, but those meanings are neither universal nor fixed. The durable question is not “what does orange mean?” It is “what role is orange playing here, and what else tells the reader about that role?” See also accessibility and data visualization.
